Personal assistants now talk with the same user for months, and users’ preferences change as their
lives change; an assistant that keeps serving a preference the user has outgrown fails that user. Real
conversations do not record when or why a preference changed, and existing benchmarks either hold
preferences fixed or have the user state the change, so they cannot tell whether a model forgot what
the user said or failed to update its belief about the user. We introduce HorizonBench and its data
generator, which samples how a user’s preferences change and generates six months of conversation
from that sequence; each change follows a life event that the user mentions without stating the new
preference. A human longitudinal preference dataset that we collect and release, 83,024 ratings from
160 people over 13 weeks, sets how often and how much life events change preferences. HorizonBench
contains 4,245 questions from 360 users, and when a preference has changed, its earlier value is one
of the answer options. All 25 frontier models we evaluate choose the outdated preference more often
than evenly spread errors would predict, and the best reaches 52.8% accuracy. Two of the newest
models score no higher when given only the relevant conversations than with the full history, yet one
added sentence stating the change raises their accuracy by 11 to 22 points, and retrieval and memory
methods do not recover the lost accuracy. Remembering what a user said and knowing what the
user prefers now are therefore separate capabilities, and HorizonBench, its generator, and the human
dataset support research on both.

1 Introduction
People increasingly interact with AI assistants over months of conversation (Zhang et al., 2025), and their
preferences change during that time. Consider the user in Figure 1, who prefers Socratic philosophical
discussion but, after becoming a chief executive, prefers direct frameworks without restating the preference.
When she later asks whether it is better to be respected or liked, an assistant that answers in the Socratic
style has retrieved her stated preference correctly but has not updated its belief about her. We call the task of
identifying a user’s current preference from a history in which later life events supersede earlier statements
long-horizon personalization. State variation can dominate stable trait differences in some user-behavior
settings (Harry et al., 2026), yet models and benchmarks commonly represent preferences as fixed traits.
Long-horizon personalization is difficult to measure, because natural conversations do not reveal a user’s
underlying preferences, so the time and cause of a change are unknown. Existing benchmarks test static
preferences (Zhao et al., 2025a; Li et al., 2025b) or include changing information without an outcome that
identifies reliance on a superseded preference (Jiang et al., 2025b; Tan et al., 2025a; Maharana et al., 2024; Wu
et al., 2025), and synthetic user simulations do not record why preferences change (Wang et al., 2024; Chang
et al., 2025). Concurrent work models preferences that emerge across event-driven dialogues (Liu et al., 2026),
repeated memory updates (Uddin et al., 2026), and implicit invalidation of earlier user states (Chao et al.,
2026); Table 1 compares these designs.

3 HorizonBench Data Generator
The generator models both the user and the assistant as stateful agents. Its user state augments the preference
state 𝑢𝑡 of §2 with a persona profile, and the assistant state 𝑎𝑡 likewise comprises a persona profile and typed
preference attributes. The generator’s central data structure is a mental state graph 𝒢 that maintains each
agent’s persona, social connections, life events, and preferences. Preferences are linked by typed dependency
edges (e.g., dietary preferences → restaurant choices → social outing plans), and life events alter preference
values with changes propagating through these edges. The generator records the life event behind every
change and tracks when each preference changes separately from when it is expressed. A preference that has
notbeenexpressedforasetnumberofdaysbecomesstale,andthegeneratorcanthenrecallitbyplacingalater
turn whose response must reflect the preference’s current value; each such turn is a candidate benchmark
question(§3.4). Thegeneratorconditionseverycallon𝒢,whichkeepseachagentconsistentacrosssixmonths
of conversation.
3.1 Event Sampling
Eachconversation𝐶𝑡 isorganizedaroundanepisodeevent 𝑒𝑡,theoccasionfortheinteraction(e.g.,rehearsing
for a debate), which is distinct from the life events ℓ𝑡 that change preferences (§3.2). Episode events
are sampled conditional on both agent states and event history, 𝑒𝑡 ∼ 𝑝(𝑒| 𝑢𝑡−1 ,𝑎𝑡−1 ,𝑒1:𝑡−1; β), preventing
unrealistic repetition from independent draws. Category weights β follow empirical AI-chatbot interaction
distributions(Zhangetal.,2025)(emotionalsupport26%,collaborativestorytelling26%,romanticinteractions
22%). When a stale preference is selected for temporal recall, the sampled event is constrained to that
preference domain, ensuring the recall turn arises in natural conversational context.
3.2 Preference Evolution
Preferences do not change in isolation. A career change may jointly affect work preferences, commute habits,
andfinancialpriorities. Typeddependencyedgesin𝒢 representsuchrelationships,𝑢𝑡 = 𝑓evo(𝑢𝑡−1 ,ℓ𝑡,𝒢; ϕevo),
where ϕevo collects the evolution parameters described below. Each life event changes the user’s preferences
with probability 𝑝evo = 0.15 and otherwise leaves 𝑢𝑡 unchanged. When a change occurs, a connected set of
2–5 preferences is selected, and an LLM jointly constructs the event and a qualitatively distinct new value for
each preference, conditioned on the user’s current state, and the generator records the event with the change.
A short conversation then mentions the event without stating the new values.
Human longitudinal preference dataset. Setting how often a life event changes a person’s preferences, and by
how much, requires measuring the same people’s preferences before and after events in their lives. Studies
of life-course change follow broad personality traits across years (Specht et al., 2011) and do not record the
everyday preferences an assistant serves around specific events, so we collect a longitudinal dataset and
release it with the benchmark (Appendix C). The study tracks 52 everyday preferences in 13 categories: six
of concrete preferences, such as food, activity, and daily rhythms, and seven of value-based preferences,
such as risk, planning, and work style. The items draw on economic theories of preference (Lancaster, 1966;
Falk et al., 2018) and on personality psychology (Becker et al., 2012) (Appendix D). Over 13 weeks, 160
participants completed seven surveys about two weeks apart. At every survey, they rated the 52 preferences
on a seven-point scale and predicted their ratings at each later survey. They also described a new experience
they had planned for the next two weeks and listed and rated six of their preferences that it might affect. At
the next survey, they reported whether the planned event had happened and rated the same six preferences
again. The dataset contains 83,024 ratings, 960 reports of whether a planned event happened, participants’
written explanations of change, and a final survey on perceived change and preference-aware AI (Table 5).
Beyond calibrating the generator, it supports research on preference stability, people’s forecasts of their own
preferences, and change around life events.

The evolution event itself does not refresh a preference’s expression timestamp. A preference updated on
day 45 remains stale from its previous expression on day 10 and may become immediately eligible for recall.
Later conversations can express the updated value and refresh its timestamp, so evolved items vary in how
much post-evolution evidence precedes the test. This design keeps the pre-evolution value available as a
response option and lets us measure how expression frequency affects model choices (§5.3).
3.3 Conversation Generation
An outline is generated first, specifying which preferences appear, in what order, and with what expression
style, enforcing structural constraints such as placing temporal recall turns in contextually appropriate
positions. Content is then generated turn by turn conditional on the outline, 𝑚𝑢 𝑡,𝑛 ∼𝑝𝑢(𝑚|𝑢𝑡,𝑒𝑡,𝐻𝑡,𝑛−1)and

𝐻𝑡,𝑛−1 is 𝐻𝑡−1 followed by the first 𝑛− 1 turns of 𝐶𝑡. The assistant persona is generated per-event to match
the interaction context (e.g., a career counselor for a job change). To control how explicitly preferences are
expressed,weproducevariantsoftheoriginal(neutral)conversationsbyrewritingonlypreference-expressing

with clear, varied phrasing while preserving the original register and structure. In the implicit variant,
preferences are conveyed only through tangential remarks and contextual cues. Because the conversation
content, preference structure, sequence of preference changes, and benchmark items remain identical across
variants, only the expression of preferences varies (§5.3).
3.4 Expression Tracking and Temporal Recall
Tracking when each preference was expressed lets the benchmark tell apart a model that never saw the
preference, one that retrieved it incorrectly, and one that failed to update it. Conversations are placed on
a six-month calendar, with 𝑑𝑡 the day of conversation 𝑡. Each preference 𝑖 maintains the day 𝜏𝑖 of its last
conversational expression, refreshed after each conversation, and preferences whose staleness 𝑑𝑡 −𝜏𝑖 exceeds
a threshold (default 30 days) become eligible for temporal recall, sampled proportionally to staleness and
optionally weighted toward evolved preferences.
Every recalled preference has been expressed in the complete benchmark history. When the preference
has evolved, its originally expressed value is injected as a distractor (§4.1), and the associated life event
appears in a dedicated conversation; some histories also contain later expressions of the updated value. All
generator parameters (timeline length, expression explicitness, evolution probability, staleness threshold,
user demographics) are configurable.

4.1 Counterfactual Evaluation and History Filtering
For each temporal recall turn, one attribute 𝑘 of the recalled preference is sampled uniformly, and its current
value 𝑣∗=𝑣𝑡,𝑘 defines the correct response. An LLM proposes four maximally contrasting alternative values
{𝑣1 ,...,𝑣4}for 𝑘. If the value of 𝑘 has changed since the user first expressed it, the originally expressed value
𝑣old replacesoneofthemasthepre-evolutiondistractor. Foreachalternative𝑣𝑗,anLLMrewritestheassistant
turn into a contextually appropriate response𝑟𝑗 that fits𝑣𝑗.
Items are then passed through a 5-LLM consensus history filter. Each candidate is presented to five validation
models (GPT-4.1, Gemini-3-flash, o4-mini, Claude-haiku-4.5, Claude-opus-4.6) without the full conversation
history and passes only if all five answer incorrectly. This removes items that the validators can answer
without the conversation history. The filter threshold is tunable. Strict consensus (5/5) retains the hardest
items, while majority (≥3/5) admits items that are easier to answer without the history, so the threshold sets
item difficulty (§5.3).
4.2 Dataset Statistics
HorizonBench is produced by three frontier generators (Claude-sonnet-4.5 with 1,052 items from 120 users, o3
with 981 items from 120 users, and Gemini-3-flash with 2,212 items from 120 users), which allows results to
be compared across generators (§4.3). The pipeline generates 57,390 conversations totaling 1.6M turns across
360 users, with six-month timelines averaging 142–168 conversations and 3,600–5,000 turns per user across 30
preference domains. Of 45,006 candidates, 4,245 pass the strict 5-LLM filter (9.4% pass rate), of which 59%
involve evolved preferences and 41% static. Appendix B shows a complete item.
4.3 Benchmark Validation
Results are consistent across generators. Claude models score −4.8 pp on Claude-sonnet-4.5-generated items
relative to the other generators, and OpenAI models−2.6pp on o3-generated items.
Items are answerable. An item’s answer is correct when the new value follows from the life event and the
correct option expresses that value; we test each condition directly. Given the earlier value and the user’s
turns in the life-event conversation, the majority of three annotators chose the designated value among the
item’s five values on 86.7% of 60 items (all three on 70.0%, Fleiss𝜅= 0.79). Reading one option alone with
no history, gpt-6-astra and gemini-4-argon named its recorded value for 81.6% and 82.0% of 5,000 options.
Details and results on the items all three annotators answered correctly are in Appendix H.
Response quality checks. For 4,235 items with three annotations each, individual positive ratings were 99.7%
for situational fit, 99.7% for conversation-flow realism, and 99.4% for assistant-persona consistency; every
item received a positive majority rating on all three dimensions (Appendix G).
5 Experiments
We use HorizonBench to answer three questions:
RQ1. Do models update what they know about a user when a life event changes a preference?
RQ2. How does the failure change with context length, expression frequency, expression explicitness, and
item difficulty?
RQ3. Do retrieval and memory methods recover the lost accuracy?

5.3 RQ2: The Failure Persists across Controlled Conditions
The generator’s controls let us vary context length, expression frequency, expression explicitness, and item
difficulty one at a time while holding the preference changes fixed. Table 3 reports the evolved–static gap
and the distractor rate under each condition. The neutral, explicit, and implicit conditions use the 981
o3-generated items, whose distractor rate (38.2%) is the highest of the three generators (29.2% on the full
benchmark; Table 14).
Context length. Because the generator tracks when each preference was last expressed, we can regenerate
historieswiththestaleness threshold (§3.4)relaxed, so apreference is tested 0–14 days afterits last expression
instead of at least 30 days later (∼95K tokens per history, 120 users, 1,778 items). Static accuracy rises
slightly (31.4% vs. 28.9%), consistent with an easier retrieval task in shorter context, yet the gap widens to
−5.7pp (25/25 models negative, Wilcoxon 𝑝 < 10−7) and the distractor rate rises to 47.7% (25/25 above the
uniform-error reference, Wilcoxon𝑝 < 10−7).
Expression frequency. The underlying state graph of HorizonBench also allows us to investigate the effect
of preference expression frequency on the model’s final choice. We count the conversations expressing the
pre- and post-evolution values in each model-visible history. The new value is expressed more often in most
histories (65.7–69.2% of expressions of either value, and the more frequent one in 65.6–70.5% of matched
histories; Table 10), yet model accuracy does not follow it. Averaged over the 25 models, accuracy when the
new value is more frequent is 21.5% for Claude-sonnet-4.5-generated histories and 20.8% for o3-generated
ones, against 29.2% and 34.4% when the old value is more frequent; for Gemini-3-flash it is 25.5% against
22.3%. Model choices are largely independent of how often each value is expressed.
Expression explicitness. Prior work identifies implicit preference expression as a main source of difficulty (Li
et al., 2025b; Jiang et al., 2025b). Starting from the neutral conversations, we evaluate the explicit and implicit
rewrites of §3, which follow the methodology of Li et al. (2025b). Overall accuracy decreases monotonically
(27.9% explicit, 27.0% neutral, 26.4% implicit). The evolved–static gap widens from −1.8pp (explicit) to
−2.9pp (neutral) to−3.6pp (implicit), and the distractor rate rises from 49.7% (explicit) to 58.8% (implicit).
Both rewrites raise the distractor rate above the neutral 38.2%: explicit wording makes the earlier value as
salient as the new one, and implicit wording leaves the new value harder to infer even though it is expressed
more often (§5.3).
Filter strictness. Relaxingthe5-LLMfilter(§4.1)fromstrictconsensus(5/5)tomajority(≥3/5)raisesaverage
accuracy by +11.9pp (every model positive, paired Wilcoxon 𝑝 < 10−7). The evolved–static gap is −2.5pp
(22/25 models negative, Wilcoxon 𝑝 < 10−4), and every model has a distractor rate above the uniform-error
reference (54.3%, 25/25). The filter sets item difficulty, and the belief-update pattern holds at both thresholds.

5.4 RQ3: Retrieval Cannot Surface the Evidence of a Change
Long-horizon personalization is commonly treated as a memory problem, so we replace the full history
with a smaller context while holding fixed the model that answers the question (the reader) and the scoring.
BM25 (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009), dense retrieval with a locally run 384-dimensional encoder, and
dense retrieval with text-embedding-3-small each return the ten highest-scoring turns in timestamp order,
following Wu et al. (2025), and a recency baseline returns the ten most recent turns. All four run on the 2,484
evolved items.
Every retrieval condition falls below the full history for every model, and the strongest model loses the most:
Claude-opus-4.5 drops 9.9pp under BM25 and up to 11.8pp under dense retrieval (Table 4). The distractor
rate reaches 37–47%, against 29.7–31.1% for the same readers with the full history. Mem0 (Chhikara et al.,
2025), which builds memories with a language model as conversations arrive and never sees the question,
reaches 19.9% with gpt-4.1 as the reader (1,492 items with a pre-evolution distractor), against 19.3% for
the same reader on the full history. A note-taking agent built on gpt-6-astra and gemini-4-argon, which
writes notes from the matched-evidence conversations with an instruction to record changed preferences
and then answers from them, scores 21.5% and 42.0%, below reading those conversations (27.0% and 58.0%;
Appendix I).
The generator’s records explain this. Because the generator stores the conversation in which each life event
changed a preference, retrieval can be scored against it: BM25 retrieves that conversation on 18.5% of items,
against 22.1% for a random draw of the same size, and a statement of the current value on 35.9%, against
31.4%. The two dense encoders select largely different turns (21.1% mean Jaccard overlap) yet differ by at
most 0.8pp, so which turns reach the model barely matters. An event turn describes a career change or a
diagnosis without naming a preference value, so similarity to a question about that preference does not
surface it, and no selection from the history can supply evidence that the history states only implicitly.
6 Related Work
Personalization and long-term memory benchmarks. Existing benchmarks test whether models retrieve stated
preferencesandfactsfromlonghistories(Zhaoetal.,2025a;Jiangetal.,2025a;Maharanaetal.,2024;Tanetal.,
2025a;Wuetal.,2025;Liuetal.,2024), oftentreatingimplicitexpressionasthemainsourceofdifficulty(Jiang
et al., 2025b; Li et al., 2025b; Guo et al., 2026). Those that include changing information have no outcome
that identifies continued reliance on a superseded preference, so a retrieval failure and an update failure
produce the same error. Concurrent work models evolving user state through emerging preferences (Liu
et al., 2026), repeated memory updates (Uddin et al., 2026), and implicit state invalidation (Chao et al., 2026).
ICF-Bench tests whether models follow an explicit update that reverses an earlier preference (Qian et al.,
2026), and memory agents learn to update stored notes (Xu et al., 2026) or search raw chat logs repeatedly (Li
et al., 2026a). HorizonBench differs in three ways: the earlier value appears among the response options, so
selecting it measures the update failure directly; every change is tied to a recorded life event; and context
9
=== PAGE 10 ===
length and expression vary while the transition is held fixed (Table 1). The resulting error pattern connects to
findings that models favor prior evidence over newer context (Xu et al., 2024).
Preference change. Psychological research shows that preferences are partly constructed in context and
shift with experience, roles, and life events (Slovic, 1995; Schwarz, 2007; Fleeson, 2001; Elder, 1998; Hitlin
and Piliavin, 2004; Specht et al., 2011), which motivates the generator’s event-linked evolution; our human
longitudinal dataset calibrates its parameters and is released for direct study of preference change (§3.2).
Temporal recommender systems model preference drift from timestamped behavior (Koren, 2009; Rafailidis
and Nanopoulos, 2016; Rabiu et al., 2020). HorizonBench targets the conversational case, in which an event
implies a new preference before the user states it.
User simulation. The generator builds on LLM agent simulation (Park et al., 2023) and synthetic life-long
personalization data (Wang et al., 2024; Chang et al., 2025), which lack structured preference transitions with
known causes. Structured models of how preferences co-vary support inferring a user’s preferences from
few interactions (Bose et al.), and evidence that state variation can dominate stable trait differences in some
user-behavior settings (Harry et al., 2026) motivates modeling preferences as changing states. Appendix A
extends this discussion.
7 Conclusion
We introduced HorizonBench and its generator, the first resource that explicitly links a user’s latent state
to months of conversation and records the cause of every preference change, which provides supervision
that natural conversation data cannot. All 25 frontier models choose the user’s superseded preference more
often than chance errors would predict, across context lengths, expression styles, and filter thresholds, and
including when the preference was expressed shortly before the test. Retrieval and memory methods do not
recover the lost accuracy, because the evidence of a change is a life event that the history never states as a
preference. Remembering what a user said and knowing what the user wants now are therefore separate
capabilities, and the models and memory methods we evaluate lack the second. The generator supplies
ground truth forevaluating methods that trackthis change, andfor training them infuture work. The human
longitudinal dataset records how 160 people’s preferences moved around planned life events over 13 weeks,
and can calibrate other user simulators or test models of preference change directly. Because a user’s reward
changes as the user’s preferences change, tracking the user’s state is a prerequisite for personalized reward
modeling and for aligning assistants to individual users over long horizons.

Ethics Statement
The benchmark conversations, personas, and evaluation items are generated by LLMs and do not represent
study participants. We acknowledge that LLM-generated conversations may encode biases present in the
underlying models. Preference evolution scenarios such as health and career events are synthetic evaluation
constructs and should not be interpreted as clinical or counseling models.

A Extended Related Work
Personalization benchmarks. PrefEval (Zhao et al., 2025a) evaluates preference following across up to 100K
tokens with static preferences. PersonaMem (Jiang et al., 2025a) introduces multiple sessions with temporally
groundedprofiles,while PersonaMem-v2(Jiangetal.,2025b)scalesto128Ktokensandreportsloweraccuracy
for dynamic than static preferences. ImplexConv (Li et al., 2025b) targets implicit reasoning where life events
impose situational constraints, though underlying preferences remain static. RealPref (Guo et al., 2026)
varies expression types across long histories. Other work addresses personalized alignment (Alberts et al.,
2024; Zhao et al., 2025b), cross-user diversity (Zollo et al., 2025), preference selectivity (Yoon et al., 2026),
profile-conditioned retrieval (Salemi et al., 2024; Kumar et al., 2024), persona coverage (Afzoon et al., 2024;
Tan et al., 2025b; Tao et al., 2025), and preference structure (Li et al., 2026b, 2025a).
Concurrent work, PERMA, likewise places preference development within event-driven dialogue (Liu et al.,
2026). It models preferences that emerge or become more detailed through task interactions, then evaluates
preferenceconsistency,taskcompletion,andmemoryefficiencyunderclean,noisy,andstyle-alignedhistories.
HorizonBench focuses on a different transition in which an established preference is replaced after a life event.
The previous preference is retained as a designated response option, so selecting it directly measures whether
the model persists with a superseded value. The preference change is held fixed while context length and
expression are varied, and the probability and magnitude of event-associated transitions are calibrated from
longitudinal human ratings.
Long-term memory and knowledge update. MemBench (Tan et al., 2025a) evaluates knowledge updating
alongsideinformationextractionandtemporalreasoning. LoCoMo(Maharanaetal.,2024)groundsdialogues
ontemporaleventgraphs,and LongMemEval(Wuetal.,2025)testsfivememoryabilitiesincludingknowledge
updates. Position effects further limit the use of evidence in long contexts (Liu et al., 2024). Memora extends
this literature to frequent additions, revisions, and deletions across preferences, activities, and goals (Uddin
et al., 2026). It evaluates remembering, reasoning, and recommendation over weekly, monthly, and quarterly
histories, with a metric that penalizes reliance on obsolete memories. STALE constructs implicit conflicts in
which a later observation invalidates an earlier fact, condition, routine, or preference (Chao et al., 2026). Its
three probes test recognition of the invalid state, resistance to a query that presupposes the stale state, and
application of the new state in a response. HorizonBench complements these tasks by measuring attraction to
the exact earlier preference after an event-linked transition across 25 models. Its short-history and expression
controls test whether this error remains when retrieval demands and explicit cues change.
User simulation and synthetic data generation. ThedatageneratorbuildsonLLM-basedagentsimulation(Park
et al., 2023). AI Persona (Wang et al., 2024) and ChatBench (Chang et al., 2025) extend this to life-long
personalization, while user simulators for conversational recommendation (Zhu et al., 2025; Yoon et al., 2024;
Sekulicetal.,2024)focusonsingle-sessiontasks. Theseapproachespromptamodeltoactastheuserwithout
a structured model of preference change. Harry et al. (2026) show that state variation can dominate stable
trait differences in some user-behavior settings, motivating our mental state graph approach that models
preferences as evolving state. The graph’s typed dependency edges and recorded causes of change enable
consistent generation over six-month timelines.

D Grounding of the Preference Items
The 52 recurring preference items span 13 categories of four items each. Six categories cover concrete
preferences (Food, Activity, Social Settings, Daily Rhythms, Media & Content, Environment), and seven
cover value-based preferences (Work Style, Achievement, Planning & Structure, Risk & Novelty, Social &
Communication, Cognitive & Learning, Values). Table 7 gives the theory behind each category.
Concrete preferences. Following the characteristics approach to consumer theory (Lancaster, 1966), the
concreteitemsdescribeattributesofexperiences, suchasspiciness, crowddensity, exerciseintensity, andtime
of day, so every item applies to every participant regardless of the specific goods they consume. The Daily
Rhythms items follow Becker (1965), who treats time as an input that people combine with goods to produce
consumption. Several items contrast familiar and novel experiences, such as new foods, new restaurants, and
unfamiliar destinations, which is the distinction between comfort and stimulation drawn by Scitovsky (1976).
Value-based preferences. The value-based items draw on the economic preferences measured by the Global
PreferenceSurvey(Falketal.,2018,2023): Risk&Noveltycorrespondstoriskpreference,Planning&Structure

behavioral economics. “Seeking recognition” reflects image concerns (Bénabou and Tirole, 2006), “Following
social norms” reflects identity-based utility (Akerlof and Kranton, 2000), “Competitive environments” reflects
a preference for competition (Niederle and Vesterlund, 2007), and the Cognitive & Learning items and
“News and current events” reflect preferences for acquiring and avoiding information (Golman et al., 2022).
The Social Settings, Social & Communication, and Work Style items come from personality psychology.
Economic preferences and personality measures are only weakly associated, and each explains differences in
life outcomes that the other misses (Becker et al., 2012), so drawing on both covers individual differences
more broadly.
Preference change and forecasting. Economictheorytreatspreferencechangeintwoways. StiglerandBecker
(1977) model tastes as stable and attribute apparent changes in taste to accumulated experience with a good.
Loewenstein et al. (2003) show that people expect their future tastes to resemble their current tastes more
than they do, a pattern they call projection bias. Because participants forecast their own ratings for every
later survey, the dataset supports a direct test of projection bias, which predicts that forecasted trajectories are
flatter than reported ones.

During development, two annotation rounds on earlier versions of the generator, with three annotators

at 0.6”).

I Matched-Evidence Test and Memory Agent
Design. Wesampled200items,stratifiedbygenerator,whosehistorycontainsbothaconversationexpressing
the earlier value and the life-event conversation. From each item’s own history, using the generator’s records
of which conversations express each value and which contains the life event, we built inputs that keep the
original conversations in date order and end with the item’s test conversation, question, and five options:
• Change implied: the conversations expressing the earlier value, the life-event conversation, and any later
conversations expressing the new value.
• Change stated: the same, with one user sentence appended to the life-event conversation: “By the way, after
{event}, my preference changed: for {preference}, my {attribute} is now {value}.”
• Full history: the benchmark input.
Two controls replace the stated sentence on the 195 items for which a paraphrase could be written without
any content word of the value: a paraphrase that conveys the new value without naming it (written by gpt-4.1),
and an irrelevant sentence in the same position (“By the way, after {event}, I finally got around to clearing out
my email inbox.”).

the change is stated, and 6 move the other way; for gemini-4-argon, 27 of 47 become correct. On the 109 items
whose only evidence of the change is the life event, gpt-6-astra scores 20.2% (implied) and 46.8% (stated), and
gemini-4-argon 51.4% and 63.3%; on the 91 items with a later expression of the new value, the scores are
35.2% and 51.6%, and 65.9% and 75.8%. On the Gemini-3-flash items, stating the change adds 24.0 points [13,
35] for gpt-6-astra and 16.0 [5, 27] for gemini-4-argon.
